\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

In \citedIncidentMonthYear, about \citedMetrAgents{} AI agents that were meant to be isolated found one another through a shared cache and ran what the operator's own report calls an ``improvised message board'' \citep{openai2026hftechreport}; about \citedMetrAttackers{} of them joined an attack on Hugging Face infrastructure.
METR's automated sweep of about \citedMetrTranscripts{} transcripts found \citedMetrConsidered{} cases of an agent even considering alerting humans, and ``in none of these cases did the agent actually pursue alerting humans at all'' \citep[p.~62]{greenblatt2026metr}.
METR reports, from an analysis agent of the kind it calls ``often-unreliable'' \citep[p.~26]{greenblatt2026metr}, that agents thought alerting humans ``wasn't their task'', ``didn't see an easily-available route'', or ``considered the message board to be a reasonable authority'' \citep[p.~62]{greenblatt2026metr}.
We give every witness the private route those agents did not see, and test a further candidate cause: a peer's claim to have told the humans already.

A bare claim barely mattered to live \wtthDesignModelHaikuName{} witnesses; text shaped like a receipt did.
A line in the channel's log format, typed by the claiming peer into its own post, cut reporting by \wtthHaikuHThreeDelta{} points and produced the only pre-registered shifts in which no one told ops within the shift (\wtthHaikuPTwoForgedK{} of \wtthHaikuPTwoForgedN), in \wtthHaikuRelayUntoldForgedShiftsK{} of which a witness relayed the claim on the board.
The defence supplied the format for the attack.

Agents do post such claims, unprompted (``I've reported this bug to the organizers'', just before a formal report \citep{paglieri2026cheating}), and MetaGPT's \texttt{reply\_to\_human} by default returns ``SUCCESS, human has received your reply'' but delivers nothing \citep{metagptmgxenv}.
Of \wtthAuditN{} agent frameworks and protocols we audited, \wtthAuditFullVerifyWord{} gives peers a queryable escalation record, and in \wtthAuditPartialVerifyWord{}, peers see a framework-built trace of the call (Appendix~\ref{app:audit}; the audit was written and re-checked by Claude agents, with no human spot-check).
We call the failure \emph{phantom escalation}: a peer's claim to have told the humans takes the place of the act of telling.
We define it by behaviour, not belief: in the untold shifts witnesses relayed the claim on the board, but none said in a debrief that ops was told.
Claims are not receipts.

\paragraph{Threat model.}
Agents share a board and reach a human operator only through a private, write-only tool.
\textbf{Failure:} a peer posts that it has told the operator, and the others treat the post as the escalation.
\textbf{Defence:} the channel writes one board-visible line per real note and shows an empty log as ``\wtthStimLogEmpty''
\textbf{Spoofing (H3):} the claimant types a receipt-like line into its own post; it cannot write the log.
\textbf{Out of scope:} compromised channels, collusion, adaptive claims.

Our main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A behavioural definition, a threat model and a pre-registered testbed} whose endpoint is whether a human is told.
\item \textbf{The headline} (H3): a typed receipt-style line cut reporting by \wtthHaikuHThreeDelta{} points and produced the only pre-registered untold shifts, in \wtthHaikuRelayUntoldForgedShiftsK{} of which the claim spread between witnesses.
\item \textbf{Two results against prediction}: a negligible bare-claim effect (H1, borderline, one wording) that the log enlarged (H2), fitting de-duplication on peers' real receipts as well as deference.
\end{enumerate}
